The **RIG exploit kit** is a sophisticated and automated system designed to facilitate cyberattacks by providing
attackers with a ready-made collection of exploits, modules, and tools. It acts as a highly efficient delivery
mechanism, automating the complex process of scanning a victim’s computer and exploiting known vulnerabilities to
gain unauthorized access.

In essence, RIG turns a manual hacking effort into a highly targeted, scalable, and semi-automated operation.

Here is a detailed explanation of what the RIG exploit kit is and how it infects computers through compromised
websites.

---

## Part 1: What is the RIG Exploit Kit?

An exploit kit is a collection of separate exploit scripts, payloads, and helper modules bundled together. The RIG
kit specifically is designed for **"information gathering"** and **"exploitation."**

### Key Characteristics of RIG:

1. **Modular Structure:** RIG is not a single virus. It is a platform that contains dozens of different exploits
targeting various operating systems (Windows, Linux), browsers (Chrome, Firefox), and specific software
vulnerabilities.
2. **Automated Reconnaissance:** Before launching an attack, RIG performs extensive reconnaissance on the victim’s
machine. It checks for crucial information such as:
    *   Operating System version.
    *   Installed browser versions.
    *   Presence of specific plugins or software.
    *   Security software status (if any).
3. **Adaptive Exploitation:** Based on the gathered reconnaissance, RIG dynamically selects the most effective
exploit module to use. If the machine is running an old version of Internet Explorer, it will use an exploit
targeting that specific vulnerability.
4. **Payload Delivery:** Once a successful exploit is achieved (gaining initial access), the kit automatically
delivers the final malicious payload—such as a Remote Access Trojan (RAT), a keylogger, or ransomware—to the
computer.

## Part 2: How RIG Infects Computers Through Compromised Websites

The effectiveness of RIG lies in its ability to use a **"watering hole"** strategy, where the compromised website
serves as the initial entry point and the delivery vehicle.

The infection process can be broken down into four distinct phases:

### Phase 1: Delivery (The Compromised Website)

The attack begins when a victim visits a website that has been compromised by attackers. This compromise usually
involves injecting malicious code into the website’s scripts, advertisements, or hidden files.

* **Delivery Vectors:** The malicious code is often disguised as a legitimate ad, a pop-up, or a seemingly
harmless script embedded on a legitimate page.
* **Initial Trigger:** When the victim loads the page, the malicious script executes silently in their browser,
initiating the RIG process.

### Phase 2: Reconnaissance and Fingerprinting

Once the malicious script is running, the RIG kit immediately begins its information gathering phase. This is the
most crucial step, as it determines *how* the infection will succeed.

* **System Check:** RIG queries the victim's operating system and browser to build a "fingerprint."
* **Vulnerability Matching:** The kit cross-references this fingerprint against its internal database of known
vulnerabilities (e.g., "This victim has Windows 7 and Chrome version X. I have an exploit that works against that
specific combination.").

### Phase 3: Exploitation

Based on the successful matching of the victim’s system to a known vulnerability, RIG launches the chosen exploit.

* **Exploitation Attempt:** The exploit module sends a specially crafted piece of code to the victim’s browser or
operating system.
* **Gaining Foothold:** If the exploit is successful, it bypasses standard security measures and establishes a
remote connection or injects malicious code directly into the operating system's memory. This grants the attacker
a temporary "foothold."

### Phase 4: Payload Delivery and Execution

With a foothold secured, the final stage is the delivery of the ultimate threat.

* **Payload Transfer:** The RIG kit transfers the final malware (the payload, such as a keylogger or a RAT) from
its server to the victim's computer.
* **Execution:** The delivered payload is automatically executed, allowing the attacker to take full control of
the compromised machine, steal data, install persistent backdoors, or initiate further criminal activity.

| Phase | Action Taken by RIG | Goal for the Attacker |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **Reconnaissance** | RIG scans the victim's OS and browser details. | Determine the weakest security link
(vulnerability). |
| **Exploitation** | RIG deploys the specific exploit matching the system. | Break through the system's defenses
and gain a foothold. |
| **Payload Delivery** | The final malware (e.g., a RAT) is installed. | Establish full, persistent control over
the computer. |

## Defense and Mitigation

Defending against exploit kits like RIG requires a multi-layered approach:

1. **Browser and OS Updates:** Keeping all software, operating systems, and web browsers patched is the single
most effective defense, as this closes the specific vulnerabilities that RIG targets.
2. **Anti-Malware Software:** Using robust, actively updated antivirus and Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR)
solutions can detect and block the exploit attempts before they successfully launch.
3. **Website Security:** Websites should employ Content Security Policies (CSP) and use techniques like
Subresource Integrity (SRI) to prevent malicious scripts from executing, even if they are successfully delivered.
4. **User Awareness:** Being vigilant about suspicious links, unexpected pop-ups, and ads is critical, as the
initial delivery method is often the weakest link in the defense chain.

Here is a structured guide on how you can approach finding evidence of a RIG infection in a PCAP file using

---

## Prerequisite: Understanding the Evidence

Before you start filtering, understand what you are looking for:

*   **Delivery Traffic:** Initial HTTP or HTTPS requests to a compromised website.
*   **Exploitation Traffic:** Anomalous packets, unusual payload sizes, or protocol errors that indicate a
vulnerability was triggered.
*   **Command & Control (C2) Traffic:** Outbound connections to external, suspicious IP addresses or domains used
by the attacker to receive the final payload.

## Step 1: Initial Triage and Filtering (The Basics)

Start broad to understand the overall traffic patterns.

### 1. Filter by Protocol (The Entry Point)
Since RIG typically starts via a web browser, focus on HTTP and HTTPS traffic.

*   **Wireshark Filter:** `http` or `tcp.port == 80`
*   **Wireshark Filter:** `tls` (or `tcp.port == 443` for HTTPS)

### 2. Filter by Destination (Where is the traffic going?)
Look for connections to external IPs that are not expected (i.e., not standard web servers).

*   **Wireshark Filter:** `ip.addr != [Internal_IPs]` (Excludes traffic only going within your local network).

## Step 2: Searching for Exploitation Traces (The Hard Part)

This is where you look for the actual *malicious* actions. RIG exploits often result in specific, non-standard
behavior.

### 1. Look for Anomalous Payload Sizes
Exploits often involve sending highly structured, non-standard data packets (shellcode or serialized code) that
may have unusual lengths or encoding.

*   **Action:** Sort the packets by **Packet Length** and look for outliers that are significantly larger or
smaller than normal web traffic.

### 2. Look for Unusual HTTP Requests (The Delivery Phase)
The initial delivery might involve requests containing unusual parameters or encoded data meant to trigger the
exploit.

*   **Action:** Examine the **Packet Details** pane for HTTP requests. Look for requests with:
    *   Unusual User-Agents.
    *   Excessively long or highly encoded query strings or POST data.
    *   Requests to unexpected file paths.

### 3. Look for Protocol Errors or Non-Standard Traffic
Some exploits rely on exploiting a flaw in how the web server handles a specific protocol (e.g., an old version of
Apache or IIS).

*   **Action:** Look for packets that violate standard HTTP protocol rules. This often requires a detailed review
of the packet layers.

## Step 3: Identifying Command and Control (The C2 Phase)

If the infection succeeded, the malware needs to "phone home" to the attacker's server. This outbound traffic is a
major indicator.

### 1. Look for Suspicious Outbound Connections
Find traffic destined for IP addresses or domains that are not recognizable as legitimate services (like Google,
Microsoft, etc.).

*   **Action:** Use the **Statistics > Conversations** window. Sort by the total number of packets or bytes
transferred. Look for long-lived connections to strange external IPs.

### 2. Search for DNS Anomalies
Attackers often use DNS tunneling or DNS requests to hide their C2 communication.

*   **Wireshark Filter:** `dns`
*   **Action:** Examine DNS queries for requests to newly registered domains, high volumes of specific query
types, or extremely long domain names (a sign of data exfiltration or encoded communication).

### 3. Look for Encrypted Communication (The HTTPS Challenge)
Since modern attacks use HTTPS, you will see encrypted traffic. While you cannot see the content, you can look at
metadata:

*   **Action:** Note the **TLS Handshake** details. Look for connections using unusual TLS versions or weak cipher
suites, which can sometimes be a red flag.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **Exploitation** | Anomalous packet lengths, unusual POST data, or protocol errors. | Packet Length Sorting;

### ⚠️ Important Disclaimer

**Forensic interpretation is critical.** Simply finding an unusual connection is not proof of an exploit kit. A
packet capture alone is often insufficient for definitive proof.

To confirm a RIG infection, you must correlate the findings:

1.  **Correlate:** Does the suspicious outbound connection (C2) occur immediately after an anomalous inbound
request (Exploitation)?
2.  **Contextualize:** Does the traffic align with the known indicators of compromise (IOCs) for the specific
exploits you are looking for?
3.  **Metadata Review:** Review the source and destination IPs to see if they map to known malicious
infrastructure.
